A few days after the nine-hour surgery to remove the tumor pressing on Jamie's spine, we had allowed ourselves to hope it wouldn't actually be cancer. Then Dr. Mo confirmed multiple myeloma. A punch to the gut. One of the hardest days — a lot of unknowns. The hardest part had not yet begun.

In the weeks that followed, Dr. Mo walked us through what the science could and could not tell him. Multiple myeloma's historical prognosis partly reflects an older patient population at diagnosis; for someone Jamie's age, new medications might reshape the trajectory, but they could also be toxic in ways that would shape her quality of life for decades. Should we go on a more intense regimen? A less intense one? Should we have hope for a cure? No protocol produced the right answer. Dr. Mo held age, disease trajectory, novel therapeutics, toxicities, and Jamie's particular life — the years ahead she wanted to use, the person she wanted to be on the other side of treatment — and made a recommendation we could live with. It was a total judgment call. It was his synthesis and judgment, and our trust in him and his training, that made it survivable.

That is what a physician is trained to do: make the call when the evidence is ambiguous and the stakes are life-shaping.

I had spent the decade before that room asking where, in healthcare, impact actually lands. Right out of college I applied to medical school. That cycle didn't work out. I was young, and I took the question seriously. I started as a research assistant at a Boston teaching hospital, on a trial of whole genome sequencing in primary care. I loved the cutting-edge work. I believe in science as a way to help heal human suffering. I also sat in on audio of primary care physicians receiving genome reports from our trial, and watched most of them, most of the time, change nothing — the research-to-clinic gap, observed as it was happening. I wondered whether the impact could be made more quickly somewhere else.

I joined a digital health startup building behavior-change programs, and saw what direct impact could feel like: people changing their lives. Then I got an MBA, became a venture capitalist, and moved to an economic-mobility venture firm. I saw a pattern there I would see from two more angles — as an LNA on a dementia and hospice unit, and shadowing primary care at a federally qualified health center. Not everyone could pay for mental health. Not everyone could pay for a primary-care physician, so they ended up in a safety-net ED, released back into the street only to come back. Medicine had stayed in the back of my mind the whole time. I was continuously comparing what I was doing against it. I wandered off on another path, wondering if I'd come back to medicine naturally. What I could not do from outside was make the call for a specific patient, in a specific life, when the evidence ran out.

The night of Jamie's surgery I had stood near midnight in the basement recovery ward of the same Boston teaching hospital where I had started that research at twenty. I had wandered a long way and come right back. In the weeks after Dr. Mo's judgment call I knew, finally, what I had been delaying. It felt like time to take the next step.

I went back to school. I retook the MCAT, enrolled in CNA training, and began shadowing Dr. Follo at a federally qualified health center. Dr. Follo does primary care for patients whose situations don't fit easy answers. I watched her hold long, honest conversations with patients about their opioid use, and watched those patients meet her openness with their own. One stayed with me: a young woman who had survived early trauma, beaten an addiction, found stability, and was now in Dr. Follo's prenatal care. Dr. Follo had quarterbacked every one of those transitions. In Dr. Mo's room the judgment call had been acute. Here it was sustained — the same physician's integration of a particular life, made over years.

Working as an LNA on a dementia and hospice unit, I met Walter — a Vietnam veteran in late-stage dementia, on hospice. He needed a bath and didn't want one. "Just leave me alone," he said. I took his hand and told him I would take good care of him. His shoulders dropped. Even when there is nothing left to fix, there is a world of good still to be done. I could be helpful, and I could make a real difference. But I could not rely on my own training to shape what came next.

I also shadowed Will Murphy, a dermatologic oncologist, whose training let him read a single mark on a patient's skin, make the definitive call, and intervene upstream — before that mark became something worse.

Jamie is well now, her disease undetectable by every measure we can read, carried there by a beautiful tapestry of people whose training helped show us hope — Dr. Mooney, Dr. Mo, Dr. Ng through the weeks of radiation, the fertility doctor who opened a conversation we didn't know to ask for. I want to earn the training that makes a person useful that way — to make the call, in the room, for a specific patient, when the science itself is not yet settled. I want to give back what I was given. The call to be of service to others, and to be there when things are difficult, is where I'm meant to be.
