## Draft

It was close to midnight in the austere basement of the Boston teaching hospital. Jamie had just come through a nine-hour surgery to remove a tumor from her spine and fuse her vertebrae. The floor was empty. Her family and I went in one by one to see her — awake, talking, herself. Even there, in that quiet clinical space, there was so much personality and so much love. I stood in the same hospital I had walked into at twenty, an aspiring doctor working as a genetics researcher. I had wandered a long way and come right back to where I began. It felt like time to take the next step.

Right out of college I applied to medical school. That cycle didn't land. I was young, and I took the question seriously. The physicians I had been around looked stretched thin, and I wasn't sure that was the life I wanted. So I went to see what else could help people. I worked as a genetics researcher and kept noticing the gap between what we found and the patients it could reach. I joined a digital health startup building diabetes-prevention tools and felt what direct impact could be — and how badly payment mechanisms held it back. I got an MBA, became a venture capitalist, and then an impact investor focused on the Medicaid population. I learned technology, systems, and incentives; I learned why mental illness cycles back through emergency rooms and why a single missed paycheck can spiral into homelessness. Medicine stayed in the back of my mind the whole time. I was continuously comparing what I was doing against it. I wandered off on another path, wondering if I'd come back to medicine naturally. Jamie's illness re-energized me.

After a trip to Scotland and the neck pain we assumed was muscular, a routine scan sent us to the ED with a suspected spinal tumor; we were transferred to Boston for the nine-hour surgery that kept her from being paralyzed. When it was over, we let ourselves hope it wouldn't actually be cancer. Then Dr. Mo confirmed multiple myeloma. A punch to the gut. One of the hardest days — a lot of unknowns.

What happened next was harder to see but carried more weight. The science was genuinely ambiguous. Multiple myeloma's historical prognosis partially reflects an older patient population at diagnosis; new medications might change the picture for someone Jamie's age, but they could be toxic and shape her quality of life for decades. Should we go on a more intense regimen? A less intense one? Should we hope for a cure? Dr. Mo weighed it with us and made a recommendation we could live with. It was a total judgment call. It was his synthesis and judgment, and our trust in him and his training, that made it survivable.

That is what the depth of physician training builds toward: making the judgment call when the evidence is ambiguous and the stakes are life-shaping. Nurses, patient advocates, and the midlevels I've worked alongside do essential work. But the training is different, and only a physician is trained to integrate across enough domains — age, disease trajectory, novel therapies, toxicity, a specific patient's life — to make a recommendation a family can trust when the science itself is not yet settled. That is the role I want to earn.

After Jamie's treatment ended I went back to school. I retook the MCAT, enrolled in CNA training, and began shadowing Dr. Follo at a federally qualified health center. I watched her practice primary care for patients whose situations don't fit easy answers — patients being honest with her about their opioid use, and Dr. Follo being just as open with them about treatments they could try. One patient stayed with me: a young woman who had survived early trauma, beaten her addiction, found stability, and was now in Dr. Follo's prenatal care. Dr. Follo had quarterbacked every one of those transitions. The physician, I began to see, is the longitudinal integrator.

I became an LNA on a dementia and hospice unit. Walter was a Vietnam veteran, late-stage dementia, on hospice. He needed a bath and didn't want one. "Just leave me alone," he said. I took his hand, looked him in the eye, and said, "Walter, I'm going to take good care of you." His shoulders dropped. His grip softened. Even when there is nothing left to fix, there is a world of good still to be done. I also learned where I sat in the story. I could be helpful. I could make a real difference. But I couldn't rely on my own skills to shape a patient's health. Shadowing Will Murphy at Memorial Sloan Kettering, I saw the same point in another register: training that lets him read a single mark on someone's skin, make the definitive call, and intervene upstream before it becomes something worse.

Jamie's care was a tapestry of physicians — Dr. Mooney repairing her spine with honest optimism, Dr. Mo's synthesis, Dr. Ng through radiation, the fertility doctor who opened a conversation we didn't know we needed to have — whose training helped show us hope. I want to be part of that. I want to give back what I was given. The call to be of service to others, and to be there when things are difficult, is where I'm meant to be. I am ready to earn the depth of training that lets me do it.
