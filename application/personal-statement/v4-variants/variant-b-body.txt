A few days after the nine-hour surgery to remove the tumor from Jamie's spine, we had allowed ourselves to hope it wouldn't actually be cancer. Then Dr. Mo confirmed multiple myeloma. A punch to the gut. One of the hardest days — a lot of unknowns. What we didn't know yet was that the diagnosis was the easy part.

In the weeks that followed, Dr. Mo walked us through what the science could and could not tell him. Multiple myeloma's historical prognosis partially reflects an older patient population at diagnosis; for someone Jamie's age, new medications might reshape the trajectory, even make a cure thinkable, but they could also be toxic in ways that would shape her quality of life for decades. A more intense regimen. A less intense one. Hope for a cure, or play for more time. The evidence itself was not yet settled. No protocol produced the right answer. Dr. Mo weighed it with us and made a recommendation we could live with. It was a total judgment call. It was his synthesis and judgment, and our trust in him and his training, that made it survivable.

That is what a physician is trained to do: make the call when the evidence is ambiguous and the stakes are life-shaping.

I had spent the decade before that room trying to understand calls like it from the outside. Right out of college I applied to medical school. That cycle didn't work out. I was young, and I took the question seriously, seriously enough to go see where, in healthcare, the most useful work was getting done. As a genetics researcher, I watched clinicians translate patterns in sequences into treatment decisions, and kept noticing the distance between our findings and the patients who could have benefited. At a digital health startup, I watched a care team make daily calls for people at risk of diabetes, and saw how much of what they could do was shaped by what would and would not be paid for. I got an MBA and became a venture capitalist, then moved to impact investing, focused on the Medicaid population. I came to understand why mental illness cycles back through emergency rooms, why a missed paycheck spirals into homelessness, and why the best tools in the world can fail to reach the people they were built for. I learned to read healthcare's systems. What I could not do from outside was make the call for a specific patient, in a specific life, when the evidence ran out.

The night of Jamie's surgery, I stood by her bed near midnight in the basement recovery ward of the Boston teaching hospital I had first walked into at twenty, a genetics researcher who wanted to be a doctor. I had wandered a long way and come right back. It felt like time to take the next step.

After Jamie's treatment finished I went back to school. I retook the MCAT, enrolled in CNA training, and began shadowing Dr. Follo at a federally qualified health center. Dr. Follo does primary care for patients whose situations don't fit easy answers. I watched her hold long, honest conversations with patients about their opioid use, and watched those patients meet her openness with their own. One stayed with me: a young woman who had survived early trauma, beaten an addiction, found stability, and was now in Dr. Follo's prenatal care. Dr. Follo had quarterbacked every one of those transitions. In Dr. Mo's room the judgment had been acute and compressed into weeks. In Dr. Follo's clinic it was sustained and longitudinal — the same physician's integration of a particular life, made across years.

I also shadowed Will Murphy, a dermatologic oncologist, whose training let him read a single mark on someone's skin, make the definitive call, and intervene upstream before that mark became something worse. Three physicians, three time horizons — the acute, the sustained, the preventive — doing versions of the same thing: bringing years of training to bear on a specific patient at a specific moment when no one else in the room could carry the decision.

Jamie's care was a beautiful tapestry of people whose training helped show us hope — Dr. Mooney, Dr. Mo, Dr. Ng through the weeks of radiation, the fertility doctor who opened a conversation we didn't know to ask for. What I want is to earn the training that lets a person make those calls — in the room, for a specific patient, when the science itself is not yet settled. I want to give back what I was given. The call to be of service to others, and to be there when things are difficult, is where I'm meant to be.
