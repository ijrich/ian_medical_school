Jamie's nine-hour surgery ended near midnight, and her family walked in one by one — Jamie awake, talking, herself, under fluorescent lights in the basement recovery ward. I had first walked into this same Boston teaching hospital at twenty, a genetics researcher who wanted to be a doctor. Ten years had turned and twisted between those two walks. Standing next to Jamie's bed, I understood what I'd been delaying, and I was ready to stop.

Right out of college I applied to medical school. That cycle didn't work out. I was young, and I took the question seriously — seriously enough to go find out where, in healthcare, the most useful work was getting done. As a genetics researcher, I watched data turn into findings, and findings struggle to reach the patients they could have helped. At a digital health startup, I saw direct intervention change lives — and saw how much of what the care team could do was gated by what would and would not be paid for. I got an MBA and became a venture capitalist, then moved to impact investing, focused on the Medicaid population. I came to understand why mental illness cycles back through emergency rooms, why a missed paycheck can spiral into homelessness, and how much of what healthcare reaches people is shaped by incentives no one in the exam room controls.

By the end of the decade I had learned to read the systems that move care. What I had not done, from any of those vantage points, was stand at the head of a patient's bed and make the call for the specific person in front of me. Medicine stayed in the back of my mind the whole time. I was continuously comparing what I was doing against it. When Jamie was diagnosed, I stopped wandering.

Jamie's illness had started with neck pain and a routine scan — and ended, after a transfer to Boston and the nine-hour surgery, with us allowing ourselves, briefly, to hope it wouldn't actually be cancer. Then Dr. Mo confirmed multiple myeloma. A punch to the gut. One of the hardest days — a lot of unknowns. What happened in the weeks that followed carried more weight than the diagnosis itself.

Multiple myeloma's historical prognosis partially reflects an older patient population at diagnosis. For someone Jamie's age, new medications might reshape the trajectory — they might even make a cure thinkable — but they could also be toxic in ways that would shape her quality of life for decades. A more intense regimen. A less intense one. Hope for a cure, or play for more time. Dr. Mo weighed the considerations with us, again and again, and finally made a recommendation we could live with. It was a total judgment call. It was his synthesis and judgment, and our trust in him and his training, that made it survivable.

That call — the one no protocol could produce — is what the depth of physician training is built for. From outside the exam room I had spent a decade learning to read healthcare's systems; from inside it, I watched Dr. Mo integrate age, disease trajectory, novel therapeutics, toxicity, and Jamie's particular life into a single recommendation her family could trust when the science itself was not yet settled. That is the role I want to earn.

When Jamie finished treatment I went back to school. I retook the MCAT, enrolled in CNA training, and began shadowing Dr. Follo at a federally qualified health center. Dr. Follo does primary care for patients whose situations don't fit easy answers — patients being honest with her about their opioid use, and Dr. Follo being just as open with them about options they could try. One patient stayed with me: a young woman who had survived early trauma, beaten an addiction, found stability, and was now in Dr. Follo's prenatal care. Dr. Follo had quarterbacked every one of those transitions. In Dr. Mo's room the judgment call had been acute. Here it was sustained — the same physician's integration of a particular life, made over years.

Working as an LNA on a dementia and hospice unit, I met Walter. He was a Vietnam veteran in late-stage dementia, on hospice. He needed a bath and didn't want one. "Just leave me alone," he said. I took his hand and told him I would take good care of him. His shoulders dropped. Even when there is nothing left to fix, there is a world of good still to be done — and the people who can shape what is still fixable do so because of what the years of training make possible. I also shadowed Will Murphy, a dermatologic oncologist, whose training let him read a single mark on someone's skin, make the definitive call, and intervene before it became something worse.

Jamie's care was a beautiful tapestry of people whose training helped show us hope — Dr. Mooney's surgical precision, Dr. Mo's synthesis, Dr. Ng through the weeks of radiation, the fertility doctor who opened a conversation we didn't know to ask for. I want to be part of that. I want to give back what I was given. The call to be of service to others, and to be there when things are difficult, is where I'm meant to be.
