A few days after a nine-hour surgery to remove the tumor pressing on Jamie's spine, we had allowed ourselves to hope it wouldn't actually be cancer. Then Dr. Mo confirmed multiple myeloma. A punch to the gut. One of the hardest days — a lot of unknowns. The hardest part had not yet begun.

In the weeks that followed, Dr. Mo walked us through the shape of the decision in front of us. Multiple myeloma's historical prognosis partially reflects an older patient population at diagnosis; for someone Jamie's age, new medications might reshape the trajectory, even make a cure thinkable, but they could also be toxic in ways that would shape her quality of life for decades. A more intense regimen. A less intense one. Hope for a cure, or play for more time. The evidence itself was not yet settled. No protocol produced the right answer.

I had applied to medical school right out of college, a decade earlier, and that cycle didn't work out. I was young, and I took the question seriously. I spent the years that followed learning healthcare from the outside — as a genetics researcher, at a digital health startup, then in venture capital and impact investing focused on the Medicaid population. I came to understand why mental illness cycles back through emergency rooms, why a missed paycheck spirals into homelessness, and why the best tools in the world fail to reach the people they were built for. Medicine had stayed in the back of my mind through all of it. I had wandered off on another path, wondering if I'd come back to medicine naturally.

Now, in the room with Jamie and Dr. Mo, I could see the decision clearly and also see what my decade had not equipped me to do. From outside the exam room I had learned to read the systems that move care. From inside it, I watched Dr. Mo hold age, disease trajectory, novel therapeutics, toxicities, and Jamie's particular life — the work she wanted to return to, the years ahead she wanted to be able to use — and integrate them into a single recommendation her family could trust when the science itself was not yet settled. Dr. Mo made a call we could live with. It was a total judgment call. It was his synthesis and judgment, and our trust in him and his training, that made it survivable.

That call is what physician training is built for. It is the one I want to earn.

The night of Jamie's surgery I had stood by her bed near midnight in the basement recovery ward of the Boston teaching hospital I had first walked into at twenty, a genetics researcher who wanted to be a doctor. I had wandered a long way and come right back. In the weeks after Dr. Mo's judgment call I knew, finally, what I had been delaying. It felt like time to take the next step.

After Jamie finished treatment I went back to school. I retook the MCAT, enrolled in CNA training, and began shadowing Dr. Follo at a federally qualified health center, where I watched her practice primary care for patients whose situations don't fit easy answers — holding honest conversations with patients about their opioid use, quarterbacking a young woman from early trauma, through recovery, through stability, and into prenatal care. I shadowed Will Murphy, a dermatologic oncologist, and watched him read a single mark on a patient's skin, make the definitive call, and intervene upstream before the mark became something worse. The shape of what I was watching was the same every time — years of training brought to bear on a specific patient at a specific moment, when no one else in the room could carry the decision.

Jamie is well now, her disease undetectable by every measure we can read, carried there by a tapestry of people whose training helped show us hope — Dr. Mooney, Dr. Mo, Dr. Ng, the fertility doctor who opened a conversation we didn't know to ask for. I want to give back what I was given. The call to be of service to others, and to be there when things are difficult, is where I'm meant to be.
