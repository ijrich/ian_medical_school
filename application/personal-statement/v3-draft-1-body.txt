It was close to midnight in the austere basement of the Boston teaching hospital. Jamie had just come through a nine-hour surgery to remove a spinal tumor and fuse her spine. The floor was empty. Her family and I went in one by one to see her — awake, talking, herself. Even in that room, there was so much personality and so much love. I had first walked into this hospital at twenty, a genetics researcher who wanted to be a doctor. I had wandered a long way and come right back to where I began. It felt like time to take the next step.

Right out of college I applied to medical school. That cycle didn't work out. I was young, and I took the question seriously. The physicians I had been around looked stretched thin, and I wasn't sure that was the life I wanted. So I set out to help from the outside. I worked as a genetics researcher and kept noticing the gap between what we found and the patients it could reach. I joined a digital health startup building diabetes-prevention tools and felt what direct impact could be — and how badly payment mechanisms held it back.

I got an MBA and became a venture capitalist. Then I moved to impact investing, focused on the Medicaid population. I came to understand why mental illness cycles back through emergency rooms, why a missed paycheck can spiral into homelessness, and how much of what healthcare can do is shaped by incentives no one in the exam room controls. Medicine stayed in the back of my mind the whole time. I was continuously comparing what I was doing against it. I wandered off on another path, wondering if I'd come back to medicine naturally. When Jamie was diagnosed, I stopped wandering.

It had started with neck pain and a routine scan. Then the ED, a suspected spinal tumor, and a transfer to Boston for the surgery. When it was over, we let ourselves hope it wouldn't actually be cancer. Then Dr. Mo confirmed multiple myeloma. A punch to the gut. One of the hardest days — a lot of unknowns.

What happened next was harder to see but carried more weight. The science was genuinely ambiguous. Multiple myeloma's historical prognosis partially reflects an older patient population at diagnosis; new medications might change the picture for someone Jamie's age, but they could be toxic and shape her quality of life for decades. Should we go on a more intense regimen? A less intense one? Should we hope for a cure? Dr. Mo weighed it with us and made a recommendation we could live with. It was a total judgment call. It was his synthesis and judgment, and our trust in him and his training, that made it survivable.

That is what the depth of physician training builds toward: making the judgment call when the evidence is ambiguous and the stakes are life-shaping. Nurses, patient advocates, and the midlevels I've worked alongside do essential work. But the training is different, and only a physician is trained to integrate across enough domains — age, disease trajectory, novel therapies, toxicity, a specific patient's life — to make a recommendation a family can trust when the science itself is not yet settled. That is the role I want to earn.

After Jamie's treatment ended I went back to school. I retook the MCAT, enrolled in CNA training, and began shadowing Dr. Follo at a federally qualified health center. I watched her practice primary care for patients whose situations don't fit easy answers — patients being honest with her about their opioid use, and Dr. Follo being just as open with them about treatments they could try. One patient stayed with me: a young woman who had survived early trauma, beaten her addiction, found stability, and was now in Dr. Follo's prenatal care. Dr. Follo had quarterbacked every one of those transitions. That was what I wanted the training to let me do — hold all of it together, across years, for someone like her.

I became an LNA on a dementia and hospice unit. Walter was a Vietnam veteran, late-stage dementia, on hospice. He needed a bath and didn't want one. "Just leave me alone," he said. I took his hand, looked him in the eye, and said, "Walter, I'm going to take good care of you." His shoulders dropped. His grip softened. Even when there is nothing left to fix, there is a world of good still to be done. I could be helpful, and I could make a real difference. But I could not rely on my own training to shape a patient's health. I also shadowed Will Murphy, a dermatologic oncologist. He could look at a single mark on someone's skin, make the definitive call, and intervene upstream — before a small change became full-blown cancer.

Jamie's care was a beautiful tapestry of people whose training helped show us hope — Dr. Mooney's honest optimism, Dr. Mo's synthesis, Dr. Ng through radiation, the fertility doctor who opened a conversation we didn't know we needed to have. I want to be part of that. I want to give back what I was given. The call to be of service to others, and to be there when things are difficult, is where I'm meant to be.
