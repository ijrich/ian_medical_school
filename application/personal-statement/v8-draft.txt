A few days after the nine-hour surgery to remove the tumor pressing on Jamie's spine, we had allowed ourselves to hope it wouldn't actually be cancer. Then Dr. Mo confirmed multiple myeloma. A punch to the gut. One of the hardest days — a lot of unknowns. The hardest part had not yet begun.

In the weeks that followed, Dr. Mo walked us through what the science could and could not tell him. Multiple myeloma's historical prognosis partly reflects an older patient population at diagnosis; for someone Jamie's age, new medications might reshape the trajectory, but they could also be toxic in ways that would shape her quality of life for decades. Should we go on a more intense regimen? A less intense one? Should we have hope for a cure? No protocol produced the right answer. Dr. Mo held age, disease trajectory, novel therapeutics, toxicities, and Jamie's particular life — the years ahead she wanted to use, the person she wanted to be on the other side of treatment — and made a recommendation we could live with. It was a total judgment call. It was his synthesis and judgment, and our trust in him and his training, that made it survivable.

That is what a physician is trained to do: make the call when the evidence is ambiguous and the stakes are life-shaping.

I had spent the decade before that room asking where, in healthcare, impact actually lands. Right out of college I applied to medical school and didn't get in. I was young, and I wanted to answer honestly whether medicine was what I actually wanted. I started as a research assistant at a Boston teaching hospital, on a trial of whole genome sequencing in primary care. I loved the cutting-edge work. I believe in science as a way to help heal human suffering. I sat in on audio of primary care visits where genome reports had been delivered, and saw how little of the new science had yet found its way into day-to-day decisions — the research-to-clinic gap, observed as it was happening. I wasn't sure whether impact could land more quickly elsewhere.

I joined a digital health startup building behavior-change programs, and saw what direct impact could feel like: people changing their lives. Then I got an MBA, became a venture capitalist, and moved to an economic-mobility venture firm. I saw a pattern I would see from two more angles — as an LNA on a dementia and hospice unit, and shadowing primary care at a federally qualified health center. Not everyone could pay for mental health. Not everyone could pay for primary care, so they ended up in a safety-net ED, released back into the street only to come back. Medicine had stayed in the back of my mind the whole time. I was continuously comparing what I was doing against it. I wandered off on another path, wondering if I'd come back to medicine naturally. What I could not do from outside was make the call for a specific patient, in a specific life, when the evidence ran out.

The night of Jamie's surgery I had stood near midnight in the basement recovery ward of the same Boston teaching hospital where I had started that research at twenty. I had wandered a long way and come right back. In the weeks after Dr. Mo's judgment call I knew, finally, what I had been delaying. It felt like time to take the next step.

Taking that step meant going back to school. I retook the MCAT, enrolled in CNA training, and began shadowing Dr. Follo at a federally qualified health center. Dr. Follo does primary care for patients whose situations don't fit easy answers — unhoused, suffering from addiction, dealing with trauma. I watched her hold long, honest conversations about opioid use in the twenty minutes she had, and watched patients meet her openness with their own. One stayed with me: a young woman who had survived early trauma, beaten an addiction, found stability, and was now in Dr. Follo's prenatal care. Dr. Follo had quarterbacked every one of those transitions. In Dr. Mo's room the judgment call was acute. Here it was sustained — the same physician's integration of a particular life, made over years.

I wanted to see if I could do the work, not only watch it. I trained as an LNA on a dementia and hospice unit. I met Walter — a Vietnam veteran in late-stage dementia, on hospice. He needed a bath and didn't want one. "Just leave me alone," he said. I took his hand and told him I would take good care of him. His shoulders dropped. Even when there is nothing left to fix, there is a world of good still to be done. I could be helpful, and I could make a real difference — the work was fulfilling in a way I hadn't expected. But I could not rely on my own training to shape what came next.

Shadowing Will Murphy, a dermatologic oncologist, I watched someone who could. He read a single mark on a patient's skin, made the definitive call, and intervened upstream — before that mark became something worse.

Jamie is well now, her disease undetectable by every measure we can read, carried there by a beautiful tapestry of people whose training helped show us hope — Dr. Mooney, Dr. Mo, Dr. Ng through the weeks of radiation, the fertility doctor who opened a conversation we didn't know to ask for. I want to give back what I was given. The call to be of service to others, and to be there when things are difficult, is where I'm meant to be.
