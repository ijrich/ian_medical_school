A few days after the nine-hour surgery to remove the tumor pressing on Jamie's spine, we had allowed ourselves to hope it wouldn't actually be cancer. Then Dr. Mo confirmed multiple myeloma. A punch to the gut. One of the hardest days — a lot of unknowns. The hardest part had not yet begun.

In the weeks that followed, Dr. Mo walked us through what the science could and could not tell him. Multiple myeloma's historical prognosis partly reflects an older patient population at diagnosis; for someone Jamie's age, new medications might reshape the trajectory, but they could also be toxic in ways that would shape her quality of life for decades. Should we go on a more intense regimen? A less intense one? Should we have hope for a cure? No protocol produced the right answer. Dr. Mo held age, disease trajectory, novel therapeutics, toxicities, and Jamie's particular life — the years ahead she wanted to use, the person she wanted to be on the other side of treatment — and made a recommendation we could live with. It was a total judgment call. It was his synthesis and judgment, and our trust in him and his training, that made it survivable.

That is what a physician is trained to do: make the call when the evidence is ambiguous and the stakes are life-shaping.

I had spent the decade before that room trying to understand calls like it from the outside. Right out of college I applied to medical school. That cycle didn't work out. I was young, and I took the question seriously, seriously enough to go see where, in healthcare, the most useful work was getting done. As a genetics researcher I watched clinicians translate patterns in sequences into treatment decisions, and kept noticing the distance between our findings and the patients who could have benefited. At a digital health startup I watched a care team make daily calls for people at risk of diabetes, and saw how much of what they could do was gated by what would and would not be paid for. I got an MBA and became a venture capitalist, then moved to impact investing, focused on the Medicaid population. I came to understand why mental illness cycles back through emergency rooms, why a missed paycheck spirals into homelessness, and why the best tools in the world fail to reach the people they were built for. I learned to read healthcare's systems. What I could not do from outside was make the call for a specific patient, in a specific life, when the evidence ran out.

The night of Jamie's surgery I had stood near midnight in the basement recovery ward of the Boston teaching hospital I had first walked into at twenty, a genetics researcher who wanted to be a doctor. I had wandered a long way and come right back. In the weeks after Dr. Mo's judgment call I knew, finally, what I had been delaying. It felt like time to take the next step.

I went back to school. I retook the MCAT, enrolled in CNA training, and began shadowing Dr. Follo at a federally qualified health center. Dr. Follo does primary care for patients whose situations don't fit easy answers. I watched her hold long, honest conversations with patients about their opioid use, and watched those patients meet her openness with their own. One stayed with me: a young woman who had survived early trauma, beaten an addiction, found stability, and was now in Dr. Follo's prenatal care. Dr. Follo had quarterbacked every one of those transitions. In Dr. Mo's room the judgment call had been acute. Here it was sustained — the same physician's integration of a particular life, made over years.

Working as an LNA on a dementia and hospice unit, I met Walter — a Vietnam veteran in late-stage dementia, on hospice. He needed a bath and didn't want one. "Just leave me alone," he said. I took his hand and told him I would take good care of him. His shoulders dropped. Even when there is nothing left to fix, there is a world of good still to be done. That room taught me the other half of this work — the rooms where trajectory is no longer writable, only to be held. Judgment-under-ambiguity is what you earn for the rooms where trajectory still is.

I also shadowed Will Murphy, a dermatologic oncologist, whose training let him read a single mark on a patient's skin, make the definitive call, and intervene upstream before that mark became something worse — an earlier, preventive register of the same physician's work.

Jamie is well now, her disease undetectable by every measure we can read, carried there by a beautiful tapestry of people whose training helped show us hope — Dr. Mooney, Dr. Mo, Dr. Ng through the weeks of radiation, the fertility doctor who opened a conversation we didn't know to ask for. I want to earn the training that makes a person useful that way — to make the call, in the room, for a specific patient, when the science itself is not yet settled. I want to give back what I was given. The call to be of service to others, and to be there when things are difficult, is where I'm meant to be.
