A few days after a nine-hour surgery to remove the tumor pressing on Jamie's spine, we were just thankful she had come through it. We had allowed ourselves to hope it wouldn't actually be cancer. Then Dr. Mo confirmed multiple myeloma. A punch to the gut. One of the hardest days — a lot of unknowns. The hardest part had not yet begun.

In the weeks that followed, Dr. Mo walked us through what the science could and could not tell him. Multiple myeloma's historical prognosis partly reflects an older patient population at diagnosis; for someone Jamie's age, new medications might reshape the trajectory, but they could also be toxic in ways that would shape her quality of life for decades. Should we go on a more intense regimen? A less intense one? Should we have hope for a cure? No protocol produced the right answer. Dr. Mo held age, disease trajectory, novel therapeutics, toxicities, and Jamie's particular life — the years ahead she wanted to use, the person she wanted to be on the other side of treatment — and made a recommendation we could live with. It was a total judgment call. It was his synthesis and judgment, and our trust in him and his training, that made it survivable.

What Dr. Mo brought to that room was two things a physician's training puts together — knowing Jamie as a particular person, and carrying the call for her. I want to earn the training that lets someone do both at once.

I had spent the decade before that room looking for where help lands. Right out of college I applied to medical school and didn't get in. I was young, and I wanted to answer honestly whether medicine was what I actually wanted. I started as a research assistant at a Boston teaching hospital, on a trial of whole genome sequencing in primary care. I loved the cutting-edge work. I believe in science as a way to help heal human suffering. I sat in on audio of primary care visits where genome reports had been delivered, and saw how little of the new science had yet found its way into day-to-day decisions. I wasn't sure whether impact could land more quickly elsewhere. I joined a digital health startup building behavior-change programs, and saw what direct impact could feel like: people changing their lives. Then I got an MBA, became a venture capitalist, and moved to an economic-mobility venture firm. I saw the same pattern from that chair — not everyone could pay for mental health, not everyone could pay for primary care, so they ended up in a safety-net ED, released back into the street only to come back. Ten years from every vantage point I could find that wasn't the bedside. Medicine had stayed in the back of my mind through all of it. I wandered off on another path, wondering if I'd come back to medicine naturally. What I could not do from outside was make the call for a specific patient, in a specific life, when the evidence ran out.

Jamie's illness had started with neck pain and a routine scan. Then the ED, a suspected spinal tumor, and a transfer to Boston for the surgery. The night of the operation I stood near midnight in the basement recovery ward of the same Boston teaching hospital where I had started that research at twenty. I had wandered a long way and come right back. In the weeks after Dr. Mo's judgment call I knew, finally, what I had been delaying. It felt like time to take the next step.

Taking that step meant going back to school. I retook the MCAT, enrolled in CNA training, and worked as an LNA on a dementia and hospice unit. I wanted to see what the bedside asked of me up close. I met Walter — a Vietnam veteran in late-stage dementia, on hospice. He needed a bath and didn't want one. "Just leave me alone," he said. I took his hand and told him I would take good care of him. His shoulders dropped. Even when there is nothing left to fix, there is a world of good still to be done. The work was fulfilling in a way I hadn't expected.

I also shadowed Dr. Follo at a federally qualified health center. Dr. Follo does primary care for patients whose situations don't fit easy answers — unhoused, suffering from addiction, dealing with trauma. I watched her hold long, honest conversations about opioid use in the twenty minutes she had for each visit, and watched patients meet her openness with their own. One stayed with me: a young woman who had survived early trauma, beaten an addiction, found stability, and was now in Dr. Follo's prenatal care. Dr. Follo had quarterbacked every one of those transitions. In Dr. Mo's room the judgment call was acute. In Dr. Follo's it was sustained — the same physician's work, extended across years. Watching her was watching what I wanted to be trained to do.

Jamie is well now, her disease undetectable by every measure we can read, carried there by a beautiful tapestry of people whose training helped show us hope — Dr. Mooney, Dr. Mo, Dr. Ng through the weeks of radiation, the fertility doctor who opened a conversation we didn't know to ask for. I want to give back what I was given. The call to be of service to others, and to be there when things are difficult, is where I'm meant to be.
