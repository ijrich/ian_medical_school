Jamie had come through a nine-hour surgery a few days earlier — the tumor removed from her spine, the fusion holding. We were grateful, and we were waiting. For a few days we had allowed ourselves to hope it wouldn't actually be cancer. Then Dr. Mo confirmed multiple myeloma. A punch to the gut. One of the hardest days — a lot of unknowns. The hardest part had not yet begun.

In the weeks that followed, Dr. Mo walked us through what the science could and could not tell him. Multiple myeloma's historical prognosis partly reflects an older patient population at diagnosis; for someone Jamie's age, new medications might reshape the trajectory, but they could also be toxic in ways that would shape her quality of life for decades. Should we go on a more intense regimen? A less intense one? Should we have hope for a cure? No protocol produced the right answer. Dr. Mo held age, disease trajectory, novel therapeutics, toxicities, and Jamie's particular life — the years ahead she wanted to use, the person she wanted to be on the other side of treatment — and made a recommendation we could live with. It was a total judgment call. It was his synthesis and judgment, and our trust in him and his training, that made it survivable.

What a physician's training puts together is two things — the knowledge to read the science around a person, and the attention to read the person around the science. That is what Dr. Mo brought to that room. That is the combined work I want to earn.

I had spent the decade before that room looking at where help lands, from every vantage point that isn't the bedside. Right out of college I applied to medical school and didn't get in. I was young, and I wanted to answer honestly whether medicine was what I actually wanted. I started as a research assistant at a Boston teaching hospital, on a trial of whole genome sequencing in primary care. I loved the cutting-edge work, and I believe in science as a way to help heal human suffering. I sat in on audio of primary care visits where genome reports had been delivered, and saw how little of the new science had yet found its way into day-to-day decisions. I joined a digital health startup building behavior-change programs, and saw what direct impact could feel like: people changing their lives. Then I got an MBA, became a venture capitalist, and moved to an economic-mobility venture firm. I saw the same pattern from that chair — not everyone could pay for mental health, not everyone could pay for primary care, so they ended up in a safety-net ED, released back into the street only to come back. Medicine had stayed in the back of my mind through all of it. I wondered if I'd come back to it naturally. What I could not do from outside was make the call for a specific patient, in a specific life, when the evidence ran out.

Jamie's illness had started with neck pain and a routine scan. Then the ED, a suspected spinal tumor, and the transfer to Boston for the surgery. The night of the operation I stood near midnight in the basement recovery ward of the same Boston teaching hospital where I had started that research at twenty. I had wandered a long way and come right back. In the weeks after Dr. Mo's judgment call I knew, finally, what I had been delaying. It felt like time to take the next step.

I started by closing the distance I had kept from the bedside. I shadowed Dr. Follo at a federally qualified health center — what I had watched from outside, now up close and personal. Her patients were unhoused, suffering from addiction, dealing with trauma — more than the twenty minutes each visit allowed could solve. Her conversations were open and honest within that window, and patients met her openness with their own. One stayed with me: a young woman who had survived early trauma, beaten an addiction, found stability, and was now in Dr. Follo's prenatal care. Dr. Follo had quarterbacked every one of those transitions. What she carried, she carried across years — the physician's duet, sustained.

Watching was not the same as doing. I retook the MCAT, trained as a CNA, and spent the following months as an LNA on a dementia and hospice unit. I wanted to know what the bedside asked of me when I was the one in the room. I met Walter — a Vietnam veteran in late-stage dementia, on hospice. He needed a bath and didn't want one. "Just leave me alone," he said. I took his hand and told him I would take good care of him. His shoulders dropped. Even when there is nothing left to fix, there is a world of good still to be done. The work was fulfilling in a way I hadn't expected. At the bedside I could meet someone where they were. What I couldn't do was carry what came next.

A decade of closing that distance showed me what the duet costs: presence and training sustained for one life across years. Jamie is well now, her disease undetectable by every measure we can read. She is here because of the people who did exactly that across her arc — Dr. Mooney, Dr. Mo, Dr. Ng through weeks of radiation, the fertility doctor who opened a conversation we didn't know to ask for. I want to earn the training that let them do what they did, and to give back what I was given. The call to be of service to others, and to be there when things are difficult, is where I'm meant to be.
