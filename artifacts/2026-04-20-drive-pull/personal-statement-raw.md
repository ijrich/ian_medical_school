## Ian Richardson – Personal Statement V2.0

Years ago, I applied to medical school. I wasn't accepted. The feedback was clarifying: I could do everything I wanted to do in healthcare—systems change, technological innovation, improving how care reaches people—without an MD. The main reason to get one was if I wanted to practice clinical medicine. To see patients. To be in the room.

I took that seriously. I set out to test whether I could accomplish what I cared about without medical training. And for a decade, I gave that test everything I had.

I became a genetics researcher, studying how whole genome sequencing could improve lives. The work was fascinating—but I kept noticing the gap between our findings and the patients who might benefit from them. So I joined a digital health startup, translating research into diabetes prevention tools that helped real people change their lives. It was the closest I'd felt to direct impact—but I started to see how payment mechanisms held back so much of what we were trying to do.

I got my MBA and became a venture capitalist, then an impact investor focused on the Medicaid population. I developed a deep fluency in technology, AI, and systems—how they work, how they scale, how they fail—and spent years studying the root causes of healthcare's hardest problems and the social forces that shape them. I found hope in the entrepreneurs attacking these problems at their roots. But what moved me most was how connected they were to the people they served. They had something I was missing: a direct human connection to the work.

Each step confirmed what I'd been told years earlier—I could do meaningful work in healthcare without an MD. But something was still missing.

Then my partner was diagnosed with multiple myeloma and a spinal tumor. I watched her doctors care for her—and care for us. The surgeon who performed her spinal surgery had spent years training to do something extraordinary, and we needed every bit of that expertise in our most difficult moment. We went from being frantically searching the internet to trusting him completely—not because he was kind, though he was, but because his depth of training gave him the ability to guide us through the worst time of our lives with confidence, clarity, and real hope. I saw what a physician could be: someone whose years of preparation let them become a trusted resource when it matters most.

Something I thought I'd left behind came back. The old dream of being a physician—not as nostalgia, but as a real question. What if I belonged on that side of the relationship? I needed to find out.

So I designed a final test. I wanted to seek out the most emotionally challenging parts of medicine—to see if I could make a difference even with a fraction of the training, and if this was truly what I wanted. I shadowed at an FQHC and saw firsthand the problems faced by underinsured and Medicaid patients—and watched a physician use trust and relationship to help them heal. I volunteered on inpatient floors and sat vigil with families whose loved ones were dying. Then I became an LNA on a dementia and hospice unit, caring for patients who were completely dependent—bathing them, feeding them, holding their hands.

One moment crystallized everything. Walter was a Vietnam veteran, late-stage dementia, on hospice. He needed a bath and didn't want one. "Just leave me alone," he said. I could see his fear. I was nervous—nervous to be in that room, nervous to do the work. I took his hand, looked him in the eye, and said, "Walter, I'm going to take good care of you." His shoulders dropped. His grip softened. He gave me a small nod. There was no cure for Walter. But I learned something in that room: even when there is nothing left to fix, there is a world of good that can still be done.

The test was over. I knew I wanted to be in the room—present, connected, hands in the work. But holding Walter's hand, I couldn't help thinking: what if I had met him sixty years earlier? Not at the end, but at the beginning—when there was still time to change the course. I had seen that physician at the FQHC—helping patients make small changes today that could reshape their futures. I wanted the depth of training to be that physician. To understand the disease, to guide the decisions, to be a trusted resource when the story is still being written.

And so I come to medicine. Not as a pivot, but as a synthesis. I tested whether I could do this work without an MD. I couldn't. I want to bring everything I've learned and pair it with the clinical training to actually be in the room. To be the person patients trust in their most difficult moments.

## Ian Richardson – Personal Statement V1.0

I stood outside Walter's door, more nervous than I'd been for anything—not of the work itself, but that I would let him down. He was a Vietnam veteran, late-stage dementia, on hospice—unable to do any of his daily activities himself. Gunsmoke played in the background. He loved a good western.

Walter had been incontinent and needed a bath. As I approached, I could see his fear. Who is this guy? Can I trust him? "I don't want a bath," he said. "Just leave me alone."

I took his hand, looked him in the eye, and said, "Walter, I'm going to take good care of you." His shoulders dropped. His grip softened. He gave me a small nod.

Something passed between us in that moment—unspoken, but real. There was no cure for Walter. But there was this—two people, one afraid and one nervous, finding their way to trust. I learned something in that room: even when there is nothing left to fix, there is a world of good that can still be done.

The path to Walter's room was not direct. I spent a decade before I got there—testing hypotheses about where I could have the most impact. Each one taught me something. Each one left something out.

My first hypothesis was that I could contribute knowledge. I joined a team of genetics researchers, studying how whole genome sequencing could improve lives. The work was fascinating—but I kept noticing the gap between our findings and the patients who might benefit from them. I didn't want to just generate knowledge. I wanted to make sure it reached people. A new hypothesis emerged: maybe my place was in that gap.

So I joined a digital health startup building diabetes prevention programs. We helped real people lose weight and change their lives. It was the closest I'd felt to direct impact. But two-thirds of Americans were at risk, and we were reaching only a fraction. I started to see how payment mechanisms held back everything we were trying to do. A new hypothesis took shape: maybe I should work to change the systems that determine whether innovation reaches people at all.

I got my MBA and became a venture capitalist, investing in companies transforming healthcare. I developed a deep fluency in technology, AI, and systems—how they work, how they scale, how they fail. Then COVID hit. The innovation was real, but it was reaching the people who already had access—not the ones who needed it most. I wanted to close that gap. Same hypothesis—just a sharper focus.

So I pivoted to impact investing, focusing on the Medicaid population—people the market often ignores. I learned why the mentally ill cycle through emergency rooms with nowhere else to go. Why one missed paycheck can spiral into chronic homelessness. Why some neighborhoods trap generations. But I also found hope—in entrepreneurs attacking these problems at their roots. They had something I was missing: a direct human connection to the work.

Then life stopped asking what I wanted. My partner was diagnosed out of the blue with multiple myeloma and a spinal tumor. It was a total shock. We were terrified—reading papers, searching Google, trying to understand what we were facing. We wanted control over something that felt uncontrollable. Then we met her surgeon. He listened. He explained the path forward. He gave us someone we could trust with the person we loved most. And in doing so, he took care of us too. We put it in his hands, and we found peace. And something clicked. The world can be quite difficult and full of suffering. But doctors and nurses are the ones who shepherd us through. They help. That's it. That's the job.

A new hypothesis. A new test. I became an LNA on a dementia and hospice unit, caring for patients who were completely dependent. Many were veterans. Some were on hospice with no family left. I did everything for them—bathing, feeding, changing, turning. There was no cure to offer. Only presence, only kindness. I sat vigil with families whose loved ones were dying. I sat alone with patients who had no one. And I found what I had been missing—the chance to be the light for others, as others had been for me. But I also felt the limits of where I stood. I was meeting patients only at the end, after the diagnosis had been delivered, after the treatment decisions had been made. I could offer comfort in the final chapter. I wanted to be there for the whole story.

And so, I come to medicine. Not as a pivot, but as a synthesis. I want to contribute knowledge—to keep asking questions about how we can better understand and treat disease. I want to ensure that knowledge reaches patients—especially those the system too often ignores. I want to bring a systems perspective to how care is delivered, because I've seen how much structure and incentives shape what's possible. And I want to do all of it with my hands in the work—connected to the people I serve, present in the moments that matter most. Becoming a physician is the only way to do all of these at once. I'm ready to walk through the door.
